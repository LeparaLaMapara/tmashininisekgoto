# Ubunye Engine Part 6.10: The Same Model in Three Frameworks

*Example 10 of eleven. Runs in five minutes fifty seven.*

---

## The problem

Every team has this argument. scikit-learn or PyTorch or TensorFlow.

It is almost always settled by whoever feels most strongly, because running the
same problem three ways on the same data is a week of work nobody has, and
comparing published benchmarks means comparing three different problems on three
different datasets.

## The setup that makes the comparison mean something

One dataset. One split, decided once, shared by all three. Three training tasks.

And the part that carries the whole example: **the three `config.yaml` files are
identical, character for character.** Not similar. The same bytes.

Each `transformations.py` is four lines: name a model class, hand it to the same
training run. The entire difference between using a decision tree library and a
deep learning framework lives inside `model.py`, which is yours.

That is the strongest possible demonstration of the idea from Part 5. The engine
never learns what a framework is. If it did, these three files could not be
identical.

---

## One practical detail worth stealing

TensorFlow needs an older version of a numerical library than the other two can
tolerate. They genuinely cannot share one Python environment.

Which is exactly why they are three separate tasks rather than one task with
three branches. **The task is the unit of isolation.** Three tasks, three
environments, no arguing about which version wins, and nobody has to pin
everything to the oldest common denominator.

If you have ever had two libraries you could not install together, that is the
shape of the fix.

---

## What the real run produced

```text
champion     pytorch
score_rmse   1.3011
margin_pct   0.5
verdict      too close to call
```

PyTorch came first, by **half a percent**.

And the task refuses to call that a win.

I think this is the best thing in the whole repository, so it is worth being
explicit about why. Sorting three numbers always produces a first place. That is
arithmetic. It happens whether or not the difference means anything, and at half
a percent it does not: the winner **flips depending on which held out split you
rank on**.

So the leaderboard prints `too close to call` and says what to decide on instead:
training cost, how heavy the dependencies are, and who has to maintain it. Those
three differ enormously between these frameworks, and the accuracy does not.

A benchmark that always crowns somebody is a benchmark that will mislead you
about half the time, confidently.

## Running it

**Free Databricks workspace:** run 6.4's feature task first so the features
exist, then the four notebooks in `examples/10_ml_frameworks/notebooks/`, three
trainings and the champion selection. Five minutes fifty seven.

**On your own machine:** the build does this on every push in its own job, with
the three frameworks pinned together in one environment purely so they can
coexist for the test.

---

## What to take from this one

**Compare on one dataset and one split**, or you are comparing something else.

**The task is the unit of isolation.** Libraries that cannot share an environment
should not share a task.

**Let a benchmark say "no difference".** The verdict "these are the same, choose
on cost" is more useful than a ranking, and far more honest.
