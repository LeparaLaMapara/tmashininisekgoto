# Ubunye Engine Part 6.7: A Data Contract, Actually Enforced

*Example 07 of eleven. Runs in one minute twelve.*

---

## The problem

Data arrives broken. A price is negative. A date is in 1970. An identifier that
should always be there is empty.

There are two usual responses, and both are bad.

**Drop the bad rows quietly.** The job finishes, the tables look fine, and the
totals are wrong by however much you threw away. Nobody knows. Six months later
somebody notices the numbers never quite reconciled.

**Fail the whole job.** Now one broken row out of two million stops everything,
including all the good rows, and somebody gets woken up to look at a single
malformed date.

## The third option

Sort the rows and keep both piles.

Good rows carry on to the clean table. Bad rows go to a **quarantine** table,
which is a real table you can query, with the reason attached. And a report
records what was checked and what happened.

Nothing is deleted, nothing is hidden, and nothing stops. Somebody can look at
the quarantine tomorrow and ask why the shop in Soshanguve keeps sending
negative prices, which is a question about the world rather than about the
pipeline.

---

## Severity, which is the part people skip

Not every broken thing deserves the same response, and the distinction is
sharper than it first looks.

**A bad row is a bad row.** One negative price is one bad transaction.
Quarantine it and carry on.

**A bad shape is a broken pipe.** If the column you were expecting is gone, or
everything is suddenly null, that is not data quality, it is a structural change
upstream. Quarantining two million rows one at a time is not a sensible response
to that. The run should fail, loudly, because continuing means producing a
confident answer from something you no longer understand.

So rules carry a severity, and the two paths are genuinely different: one sets a
row aside, the other stops the run.

---

## How you know it works

Here is the part I find genuinely clever about this example.

It is easy to write quality rules that pass because there is nothing wrong. That
proves nothing. The example therefore **injects faults on purpose**: it takes
real transactions and deliberately breaks five of them, remembering exactly
which.

Then it checks two things that would otherwise be assumptions:

```python
assert not missed,  "the quarantine MISSED deliberately broken rows"
assert leaked == 0, "broken rows leaked into clean_transactions"
```

Every fault it planted must be caught, and none of them may reach the clean
table. Both directions, because a quarantine that catches everything including
good rows is just as broken as one that catches nothing.

---

## What the real run produced

```text
rules_checked        7
faults_injected      5
faults_quarantined   5
leaked_into_clean    0
```

Five in, five caught, none leaked, seven rules reported. That is a data quality
system proving itself rather than asserting itself.

## What to take from this one

**Quarantine, do not drop.** A bad row is evidence about the world. Keep it.

**Separate a bad row from a broken pipe.** One should be set aside, the other
should stop everything.

**Break your own data on purpose.** A quality check that has never caught
anything is a quality check nobody has tested.
