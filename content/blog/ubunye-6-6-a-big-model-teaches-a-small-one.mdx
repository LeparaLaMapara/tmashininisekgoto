# Ubunye Engine Part 6.6: A Big Model Teaches a Small One

*Example 06 of eleven. Runs in seventeen minutes fifty three, the longest in the
set, because a model is genuinely being trained.*

---

## The problem

You have a large language model that can read a customer review and tell you
whether it is positive or negative. It is very good at this.

It is also slow and expensive, and you have four million reviews arriving every
month, forever.

Running the large model on all of them costs more than the answer is worth. And
the task itself is not hard. It is the kind of thing a much smaller model could
do, if only somebody had labelled enough examples to train it on, which nobody
has.

---

## What it looks like

![Example 06: a large model labels reviews once, a small model trains on those labels, gated on catching the negatives](/posts/ubunye_ex06_architecture.png)

---

## The move

Use the expensive model **once**, not forever.

Run it over a few thousand reviews and write down what it says. That gives you
labelled training data that did not exist this morning. Then train a small model
on those labels, and from then on the small one does the work.

The large model was the teacher. The small one is the student. This is called
distillation, and the name is apt: you are extracting the part of a large thing
that you actually need.

What makes it worth doing is the economics. The teacher runs once, over a sample.
The student runs forever, over everything, on hardware you already have.

---

## The gate, and why it reads an unusual number

The student gets the same treatment as any other model, because it is a model:
trained, judged on data it never saw, gated, registered only if it passes.

But the gate here does not read overall accuracy, and the reason is worth
understanding because it applies to most real classification problems.

Most reviews are positive. If nine in ten are positive, a model that answers
"positive" to absolutely everything, including furious complaints, scores **90%
accuracy**. It looks excellent on the headline number and it is completely
useless, because the only reviews anyone cares about are the negative ones.

So the gate reads the number that exposes that: how many of the genuinely
negative reviews the model actually caught. A model that answers positive to
everything scores zero on that, and never gets registered.

**Pick the number that a useless model would fail.** If your metric cannot tell
the difference between your model and a model that always says the same thing,
it is the wrong metric.

---

## What the real run produced

```text
agreement_with_teacher   0.974
held_out_reviews         38
classes_predicted        ['negative', 'positive']
```

The student agreed with its teacher on **97.4%** of reviews neither had been
trained on. And `classes_predicted` is there for exactly the failure above: it
proves the student actually produces both answers, rather than having learned to
say one word.

Those are small numbers of reviews, and that is honest. This is an example that
has to run in a free workspace in under twenty minutes, not a production system.
The process is the point, and the process is identical at four million.

---

## Running it

**Free Databricks workspace:** the two notebooks in
`examples/06_finetune_llm/notebooks/`, in order. **Give it twenty minutes.** It is
the slowest example in the set and it is training a real model, not pretending
to.

**On your own machine:** the teacher becomes a small open model instead of a
hosted one, and the student stays exactly what it was. Distillation is teacher to
student, and neither of them has to be hosted by anyone. The build runs it that
way on every push.

---

## What to take from this one

**Expensive once, cheap forever.** A large model is often best used as a source
of training data rather than as the thing in the hot path.

**Labelled data can be manufactured.** The reason you could not train a small
model was usually that nobody labelled anything. Now something can.

**Gate on the number a lazy model would fail.** Overall accuracy hides the
failure that matters when your classes are unbalanced, which they usually are.
