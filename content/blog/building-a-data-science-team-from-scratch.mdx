# Building a Data Science Capability in Under Two Years: A Real Life Roadmap

> I did not build a data science team from scratch. I inherited one. There were
> capable people already carrying real production work for real parts of the
> business. What was missing was the system around them: there was no handover
> and no useful documentation, demand arrived from everywhere and became work
> immediately, the important knowledge lived in individual heads, there was no
> dedicated data engineering, deployment happened by hand, and the infrastructure
> underneath had been built for a different kind of work. This is the honest
> account of building that capability, in under two years, including the part
> where I was the bottleneck.

## The system I inherited

Picture the first month. The models are real. They run in production, the
environment processes roughly five million records a day, and parts of the
business make decisions with the outputs. The people are real too, and good.
What did not exist was almost everything between those two facts.

Now the part I want to be exact about. There was no handover. There was no
useful documentation for the systems and models I inherited. Not thin
documentation, not out of date documentation. None. So I had to work out what
was running in production while helping keep it running, and some problems were
already happening while I learned.

Requests arrived from wherever they arrived and became work the same afternoon.
The team had grown out of analytics and business analytics rather than
engineering, so there was deep domain knowledge and very little dedicated data
engineering or MLOps capability. Deployment was an act of will each time, put
together by hand.

There was infrastructure, and that is worth saying plainly, because the lazy
version of this story is that nothing existed. We had
[Kubernetes](https://kubernetes.io/), [Jenkins](https://www.jenkins.io/) and CI
pipelines, [Microsoft SQL Server](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sql/sql-server/)
on premises, [PostgreSQL](https://www.postgresql.org/) databases and Windows
virtual machines. Some work still ran off somebody's laptop, and a few database
environments had unclear permissions and no obvious owner.

None of those technologies are bad. The problem was fit. Almost all of it was
built for applications, software that serves requests and touches a modest
amount of data at a time. Our work went the other way: move and process large
volumes, rerun history when something changed, train models, repeat reliably.
**We had plenty of places to run software. What we did not have was a good place
to do data science at scale.**

In that situation the temptation is to start fixing the loudest thing. Do not.
**The loudest thing is almost never the load bearing thing.**

I want to be precise about the diagnosis, because everything that follows
depends on it. The team was not underperforming. The system around the team was
incomplete. If you get that backwards you start replacing people, and you end up
with a new team inside the same broken system, running the same firefight with
less institutional memory than before.

Which is really one idea: **having a data science team is not the same thing as
having a data science capability.** The team was already there. The capability
around it was what we had to build.

| What I inherited | Where it is now |
| --- | --- |
| Requests arriving anywhere, worked on immediately | Demand enters an intake, is prioritised, lands in a book of work |
| No handover, no useful documentation | Written down, and used by governance and other teams |
| No dedicated data engineering | A data engineer in the team, specialists federated |
| Deployment negotiated model by model | Reusable patterns, five production models modernised |
| A stack built for hosting applications | Databricks for distributed work, cloud migration in flight |
| Roughly a quarter to get a use case live | Roughly two weeks, or closer to a month with legacy integration |

None of that came from replacing the people. It came from changing the system
around them, in an order that mattered.

## Visibility before velocity

The first problem was not technical. It was that nobody, including me, could
answer a simple question: what is this team working on, and against what
priority?

Work arrived directly. Someone who needed something asked the person they knew,
and the request became work, because saying yes is easier than having a process
that can say later. The result is a team that is permanently busy and
permanently behind, where the loudest request wins and the most valuable work is
whatever nobody interrupted.

So we built an intake. [Jira](https://www.atlassian.com/software/jira) and two week sprints are the visible part, and
honestly the least interesting part. What mattered was the shape underneath. A
request now enters an intake rather than a person's inbox. It is assessed and
prioritised against everything the team has already committed to. It lands in a
book of work, and it is pulled into a sprint when there is capacity for it.
Delivery is then visible both to the person who asked and to the people doing
the work.

![The demand flow: a request enters an intake, is prioritised against existing commitments, lands in the book of work, is pulled into a sprint and delivered, with visibility across every step](/posts/ds_demand_flow.png)

That is an operating model, not a project management tool. The difference shows
up in the conversations it makes possible. Before, the answer to "can you also
do this" was yes, or a negotiation with whoever pushed hardest. Now it is a
comparison: here is what it would displace, and what that costs. Prioritisation
stops being a personality contest.

We kept the ceremony light on purpose. Standup every second day rather than
daily, because a small team fighting production issues does not need another
meeting, it needs a rhythm. At the end of each sprint, the honest questions:
what got done, what did not, why, and who is blocked.

The first return on visibility was not speed. It was knowledge. Within a few
sprints I could see which skills we had, which we lacked, and exactly where work
went to die. You cannot design a capability mix until you can see the gaps, and
you cannot see the gaps until the work is visible.

The second return took longer to notice. Visibility is what lets a team manage
itself. A sprint is not twenty tasks with hourly updates. It is a shared
direction, a time window and agreed priorities. Once people can see the work
they know what they own, they pace themselves, and they help each other without
being told to.

That only worked because my manager gave us room. He trusted the team and did
not run the implementation from above. It is very hard to build a team that
stands on its own if the people above it will not let go, and I would not want
this read as though I did any of it alone.

## Turning tribal knowledge into organisational memory

Once the work was visible, the next fault line showed itself. Almost everything
important was tribal. It lived in the memory of whoever built it. In a small
team that is not a culture quirk, it is a single point of failure with a
resignation letter attached.

[Confluence](https://www.atlassian.com/software/confluence) was the tool.
Converting tribal knowledge into organisational knowledge was the objective, and
those are not the same project.

We started with the onboarding guide I wished someone had handed me: every tool,
where to find it, how to get access, what a regulated environment requires
before you touch anything. Then the harder work of going back into our own
production estate and reverse engineering it. What each model does. What feeds
it. Who consumes the output. Then the deployment process, the engineering
patterns, and a straight answer to the question that wastes the most time: does
this already exist somewhere in production?

The principle I keep coming back to: once a team solves the same kind of problem
repeatedly, the solution should stop living in someone's memory and become a
reusable pattern. Everything later in this post is a version of that sentence.

Richard Feynman was once told that his notebooks were a record of his thinking,
and he corrected the historian: the notes were not a record of the thinking,
they were the thinking. That is what documentation is for a team. Not admin
performed after the real work. The team thinking, in a form that survives the
team.

What I did not predict was how far it would travel. People who joined later
onboarded from a documented path instead of an oral tradition, which is the
entire point. Teams outside our area started using it to understand our models
for their own work.

Then it paid off in a way I had not planned for at all. Production models came
under audit, compliance review and model governance. Reviewers wanted to know
what a model did, how it worked, what data it used, why particular decisions had
been made, what controls existed and what evidence supported any of it.

Writing something down does not make it compliant, and I am not claiming it did.
The claim is smaller. Because we had already understood these systems and
written them down, we could walk reviewers through the models instead of
spending weeks rediscovering our own production estate under pressure.

Months earlier, documentation had felt like extra work. During audit, it became
evidence.

## Building the right capability mix

There were real gaps in capability and capacity, and some of them could not be
trained away in the time available.

The first was somebody who already worked in the organisation. They had strong
data management knowledge and, more to the point, they knew the place: where
data lived, which databases mattered, how the business actually used them. Given
the opportunity, engineering direction, support and real responsibility, they
grew into the data engineering role. The team had never had dedicated data
engineering, and it showed in the places you would expect: pipelines built as an
appendix to a model, ingestion solved from first principles every time. Someone
who already knew where the data lived was worth more than a stronger generalist
would have been, because the scarce thing was context, not competence. Building
capability means developing people, not only installing technology.

The second was a junior data scientist, and the reasoning was capacity
rather than seniority. A meaningful part of our demand was recurring and ad hoc
analysis: genuinely valuable to the business, effectively endless, and corrosive
to any attempt at sustained production work. Absorbing it deliberately created
room for the rest of the team to do the engineering heavy work.

What we did not do was try to hire every capability we might occasionally need.
Budget was a real constraint, but it is not the interesting part. Permanently
owning a specialist capability you need twice a year is poor organisational
design on its own terms. It creates underused expertise and quietly duplicates a
function the organisation already has.

So where deep specialist expertise was required and permanent headcount was not
justified, we worked with the central technology and capability teams and
brought people in for the specific problem: platform engineering, data
engineering, AI engineering, solution architecture and the central AI
capability.

That only works if the relationships exist before you need them. I spent real
time building them with platform engineers, data engineers and technical leads
elsewhere who had solved problems like ours, and we lined up on shared technical
and business goals rather than on org charts. **You do not need everyone to
report to you to build something together.** What makes it work is not a process.
It is trust, honest communication, and being technically credible enough that
people want to help you.

![The capability model: a core team of data science, data engineering and analysis, alongside federated central capability in platform engineering, AI engineering, solution architecture and central AI](/posts/ds_capability_mix.png)

The lesson took me longer to learn than it should have. Building capability does
not mean owning every capability. You have to decide deliberately what lives
permanently inside the team, what the team develops, and what is shared across
the organisation. Own everything and you build a silo you cannot sustain. Share
everything and you have no capability of your own.

## The day 250 million records exposed the infrastructure

Then reality presented the bill for infrastructure decisions made long before I
arrived.

The business migrated to a new core policy administration environment. Things
broke, and one of our products began accumulating unprocessed records. By the
time it was contained, the backlog was roughly 250 million records, against a
normal daily volume of roughly five million. On the existing stack, working
through that took roughly three to four months.

The part people underestimate is that the data does not wait for you. It kept
arriving at the same rate the whole time, so every day we did not catch up,
another day of records joined the queue behind the ones already there.

```text
arriving faster than we process
   -> the backlog grows

same rate in, same rate out
   -> it stops growing
   -> but it never shrinks

processing faster than it arrives
   -> the backlog finally clears
```

We tried the obvious thing first, which was to make the existing setup go
faster. It was not enough, and that is the useful part of the story, because it
proved the problem was not effort or tuning. Every hour we spent being clever
inside that constraint was an hour spent proving the constraint.

It was not only a technical inconvenience. Clients complained, the business
pushed hard and reasonably so, cancellations were happening around the wider
issue, and people were under real stress for weeks.

I had seen it coming, and this is the most transferable thing I know:
**you do not get budget for infrastructure. You get budget for pain.**

When the backlog arrived I did not bring management a complaint. I brought a
costed proposal that was already written, and then I carried it end to end: the
commercial case, standing the environment up with the platform and networking
teams, and a migration path off the legacy stack that other workloads could
follow afterwards.

Once that workload moved into Databricks, a comparable volume could be processed
in under a day.

![The processing comparison: roughly 250 million records took three to four months on the old stack, and a comparable workload runs in under a day on Databricks](/posts/ds_backlog_processing.png)

I want to be careful with that comparison, because it is the strongest number
here and easy to overstate. It is not a benchmark. It is the same class of
workload on infrastructure that matches its shape instead of resisting it. The
strategy argument ended there, which is the only way arguments like that end.

The migration left a second problem, quieter and in some ways worse. It also
introduced inconsistencies in the data itself. Some of those values feed
calculations with real financial consequences, including payouts. I am not
claiming wrong payments went out. I am saying it was a risk we had to control
quickly.

So the job was never simply "process the backlog". It was three things at once:
clear the backlog, keep up with what still arrived daily, and make sure the data
could be trusted before anything downstream used it. That third
one is what quietly set the engineering agenda: proper ingestion and ETL, checks
that catch bad values before they spread, reconciliation so we could prove a
number, monitoring so we heard about problems from our systems rather than from
a client, lineage, and the ability to safely reprocess history.

**Scale is not only about processing more data. It is about processing more data
without losing control of it.**

Which taught me the thing I actually took from this period. I could have spent
it improving how we built models and it would not have helped, because the
models were not the weak part. Data scientists cannot spend their time modelling
if they are forever solving ingestion, access, transformation and quality by
hand. **At scale, data science is only as reliable as the data foundation
underneath it.** That is why the investment went into data engineering first.

It is fair to ask how I knew where to look. Before this role I worked at IBM and
Vodacom, where data science was genuinely engineered and large scale processing
was treated as its own discipline. I did not copy them and they were not
perfect, but I had seen what a stronger foundation looks like. At IBM and
Vodacom I learned what a more mature capability could look like. Here I learned
what it takes to help build one.

## Databricks and the wider modernisation

I introduced Databricks into the team's environment and led its adoption, and I
want to be exact about what that means, because the version of this story where
one person single handedly replatforms an enterprise is a fiction that helps
nobody. I did not do every infrastructure task. I identified the capability we
needed, set and defended the direction, and worked across the specialist teams
who own those disciplines. The engineering was collective. The direction and the
accountability were mine.

During the worst of that pressure I also built the first version of something we
needed over and over: a reliable way to get data out of our on premises systems
into the cloud data environment and catalog over
[JDBC](https://docs.oracle.com/javase/tutorial/jdbc/). Once it worked on
something real, it was written down and taught, so the next person did not have
to solve it again from first principles.

```text
understand the problem
  -> design an approach
  -> build the first one
  -> prove it on real work
  -> write it down
  -> teach it
  -> the team adopts it
  -> repeat and improve it
  -> hand over ownership
```

The point was never for me to become the person who moves every dataset forever.
If I solve something once, the team should not need me to solve it again.

The wider modernisation is bigger than any one product, and it is still in
motion:

- from on premise infrastructure toward AWS
- from on premise deployment services toward managed cloud equivalents
- away from fragmented deployment patterns built around Jenkins and Kubernetes,
  where those are not the right fit for data science workloads
- toward a more integrated, Databricks centric environment for data science and
  machine learning

I am deliberately not writing that in the past tense. Some of it is done, some
is in flight, and some legacy components are still exactly where they were,
because they hold real things up and nobody sensible removes those on a schedule
that suits a blog post. A modernisation that claims to be finished is usually
one that has not been inventoried.

## From one off deployments to reusable patterns

Underneath the infrastructure problem was an engineering one, and it was the
more expensive of the two.

There was no reusable path to production. Each use case negotiated its own way
there: its own deployment, its own scheduling, its own integration decisions,
its own opinion about where configuration belongs and how credentials are
handled, much of it manual work around Jenkins on infrastructure never designed
for distributed data science. It worked, in the sense that things ran. It also
meant every model paid the full engineering cost of being the first model.

That is why a use case could consume roughly a full quarter before it was
properly in production. Almost none of that quarter was the data science.

So we built the layer that was missing: ingestion patterns, model development
patterns, deployment patterns, dashboarding patterns, and CI/CD through GitHub
workflows, with governance, reproducibility and access control designed in
rather than bolted on afterwards.

The lesson is not subtle, but it took a backlog to make it undeniable. Reusable
patterns compress delivery time because teams stop rebuilding the same
infrastructure and deployment decisions for every model. You are not buying
cleverness. You are buying the right to stop making the same twenty decisions
again.

The stronger foundation also changed how we did the data science itself, which
is the outcome I care about most. Work moved into source control so another
person could pick it up and run it. Reproducibility improved, with testing where
it earned its place. We built repeatable ways to develop, deploy and move models
from experiment toward production, with CI/CD through
[GitHub Actions](https://docs.github.com/en/actions) and monitoring designed in
rather than bolted on. We could finally standardise how data science reached
production instead of treating every project as a new engineering problem.

That same foundation now carries ingestion, ETL, transformation, model
workloads, deployment and orchestration, and increasingly LLM and agent
workloads too. One foundation carrying many kinds of work is exactly what you
lose when every project invents its own.

Which is worth being blunt about, because the platform is the part people
fixate on and the smallest part of it:

```text
           PEOPLE
   skills, trust, ownership
              |
          PRACTICES
   documentation, standards
      testing, CI/CD
              |
        DATA SCIENCE
   experiments, models
   deployment, monitoring
              |
      DATA ENGINEERING
   ingestion, ETL, quality
    pipelines, lineage
              |
          PLATFORM
   compute, storage, catalog
       access, cloud
```

Take any layer out and the ones above it wobble.

## Where Ubunye Engine fitted

[Ubunye Engine](https://ubunye-ai-ecosystems.github.io/ubunye_engine/) did not
start at work. It began in my own research, where I kept rebuilding the same
scaffolding for every experiment and got tired of it, and I carried on
developing it independently afterwards. It is open source and available on
[PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/ubunye-engine/). You describe a data or machine
learning pipeline once, as configuration and a small amount of Python, and run
that same definition across different execution environments. I mention the
history only because provenance is worth being accurate about.

I am aware of how it looks. A manager who builds something and then introduces
it to his own team is one bad decision away from vanity engineering. The test I
held it to was simple: it had to solve a problem the team already had, and it
had to survive other engineers looking at it.

The problem it speaks to is the one in the previous section. We needed a
standard engineering pattern instead of another one off implementation per use
case, and the ideas I had worked out building it shaped how I approached that.
There was a second reason that mattered as much: the team came from analytics
backgrounds rather than platform engineering, and telling people to adopt strong
conventions in the abstract achieves very little, while giving them a concrete
pattern to work inside achieves a great deal. Conventions teach.

Then we took it outside the team, which is the part that makes it real, so the
patterns could be challenged as well as adopted. Challenged is the important
word. A pattern only ever reviewed by the person who wrote it is a preference,
not a standard.

Databricks and Ubunye are complementary, and the boundary is worth stating
plainly. A **platform** is compute, storage, catalog and execution. A
**framework** is the conventions and reusable ways you structure and run work on
top of it. Databricks is the first, Ubunye is the second. It does not replace
Databricks, platform engineering, Kubernetes, CI/CD or architecture, and any
explanation that implies otherwise is selling you something.

Five production models have been modernised onto the newer patterns so far. The
number I care about is not the total running in production. It is that existing
production workloads are actively moving into a more standardised, scalable and
maintainable architecture instead of accumulating in place.

The engine's own story, from why convention is the real deliverable through to worked examples, is told in [the Ubunye Engine documentation](https://ubunye-ai-ecosystems.github.io/ubunye_engine/).

## Developing people instead of hiring around problems

Every structural change in this post assumes the team can operate it. That
assumption only holds if you invest in the people you have.

The mechanism we kept is a show and tell every Friday, and the rules are loose
on purpose. Bring the model you are building. Bring something you learned about
the business. Bring a technology you are curious about, a lesson from a course
the company is sponsoring, an engineering practice you want to argue for, an
experiment that worked, or one that did not. Failures count, and saying so out
loud is most of what makes it work.

The purpose is horizontal knowledge movement. In a small specialised team,
expertise concentrates naturally: one person becomes the one who understands
that model, that pipeline, that integration. A weekly show and tell erodes that
on purpose, until everyone can do a bit of everyone's job. That is the only real
insurance a small team has against key person dependency, and it is a training
programme with a budget of zero.

The rest of the teaching was less formal and just as deliberate: Databricks,
[PySpark](https://spark.apache.org/docs/latest/api/python/), the engineering
patterns, the documentation habit, how deployment actually works. The goal was
never for me to be the person who knows everything. It was for anything I
learned or solved once to become ordinary team knowledge. I understand it, I
prove it, I explain it, somebody else uses it, and eventually nobody remembers
it was ever a special skill.

Like the documentation, it outgrew us. People from across the business now
attend and give feedback, which quietly turned a team ritual into a standing
relationship with our stakeholders.

## Federating capability across the organisation

There is a difference between building capability and hoarding it.

Some of what we built early, we built because nobody else was going to. That is
defensible at the time and indefensible as a permanent arrangement, because a
small data science team maintaining its own engineering platform is a small data
science team that has stopped doing data science.

So we handed capabilities outward, deliberately, to the teams whose job they
properly are, and worked with platform engineering, solution architecture and
the central AI capability so that what we depend on is supported by people who
specialise in supporting it. Team Topologies has the cleanest language for this:
platform teams exist so that stream aligned teams can flow.

Federation is also how a pattern survives you. Anything adopted only inside one
team dies when that team reorganises, and teams always reorganise.

**Good technical leadership is not owning everything forever. Sometimes you
build what is missing, prove it, teach it, and then give it to the team that
should own it.** That is how the data science team gets its capacity back for
actual data science.

## What changed in delivery velocity

Numbers, with their caveats attached, because operational observations are not
service level agreements.

Historically, getting a use case properly deployed could consume roughly a full
quarter, most of it engineering and integration work that somebody had already
done once before.

With the newer patterns, a reasonably straightforward use case without
significant legacy integration typically moves from prototype toward production
in roughly two weeks. Where substantial legacy system integration is involved,
it is closer to a month. The variable is almost always integration complexity
rather than the modelling.

Recently we delivered at least three use cases inside a single month. Through
the old approach, a comparable set of work could have consumed a significant
part of a year. I am not going to convert that into a saving, because the honest
claim is about order of magnitude, not a precise figure.

The change I value most is not on that list. The team stopped being reactive. We
used to chase whatever was on fire that day. We now choose most of our work,
which is why the capability supports a growing set of use cases across multiple
business areas rather than a queue of interruptions.

We are not finished, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Some foundational
ownership is still moving toward the teams that should hold it long term, and
there is plenty left to improve. But our data scientists now work on this
platform and have adopted the patterns and the thinking. It is increasingly how
the team works rather than how I think we should work, and that is the only
version of this that lasts.

## Learning to influence instead of insisting I was right

The hardest part of the two years was not technical, and the way I used to tell
this story was wrong.

I used to frame it as holding a line against management. That framing is
flattering and it is useless. What is actually happening is that two groups are
optimising over different time horizons. Management needs delivery it can
measure this quarter, which is a reasonable requirement and not an obstacle.
Engineering can see investments in infrastructure, governance and capability
whose returns arrive over years. Both views are legitimate. Neither is complete
on its own.

My job was not to win. It was to translate. An infrastructure investment does
not become fundable because it is architecturally correct. It becomes fundable
when it is expressed in terms the organisation can evaluate: delivery time,
operational risk, scalability, reliability, cost and business impact.

Sometimes that translation changed my mind about my own proposal, and the
technical answer that survived contact with a cost constraint was better than
the one I walked in with. Other times it meant waiting and gathering operational
evidence until the investment was obvious to everyone rather than only to me.
The Databricks decision is the clearest example. I did not win that argument by
being right about distributed processing. I won it when the pain had a number
attached to it, and I had the proposal ready when it did.


## Building myself out of the critical path

Now the part I got wrong.

For a stretch, I was holding too much of it together personally: the process,
the technical direction, the engineering standard and delivery. It felt like
leadership. It was a design flaw, and the way to detect it is uncomfortable. Ask
what stalls if you are away for two weeks. If the answer is a list, then what
you have built depends on you, and a capability that depends on one person is
exactly the thing I claimed to be replacing.

Getting out of the way is not a single decision. It is a progression, and it is
the one I would use again.

![The leadership progression: solve the problem, turn it into a pattern, teach the pattern, federate it into the wider organisation, then step out of the critical path](/posts/ds_leadership_stages.png)

We are not uniformly at the last stage, and I would not believe anyone who claimed
their team was. Different parts of the capability sit at different stages. The
operating model and the documentation are furthest along. Some engineering
patterns are somewhere around stage four. There are corners where the honest
answer is still stage one. Knowing which is which is most of the job.

Bringing in a project manager and scrum masters to run the process was part of
this, and so was handing platform work to platform people. The cost of this
phase is ego, and it is the cheapest price on this page once you understand what
it buys.

I used to think the point of leading was being the person who could always fix
it. I now think it is the opposite. The measure of the job is whether the team
can say they did it themselves and be right about it.

## What I would carry into the next team

1. **Diagnose the system before you judge the people.** Capable people inside an
   incomplete system look exactly like an underperforming team, and if you read
   it backwards you will replace the wrong thing.
2. **Make demand visible before you try to make delivery fast.** You cannot
   prioritise what you cannot see, and you cannot design a capability mix until
   you can see the gaps.
3. **Documentation is how a team remembers.** Once you have solved the same kind
   of problem twice, the solution belongs to the organisation, not to a person.
4. **Building capability does not mean owning every capability.** Decide what
   lives permanently inside the team, what you develop internally, and what you
   federate. Both extremes are expensive.
5. **Write the costed proposal before the crisis.** Funding follows visible
   pain, so have the answer ready for the day the pain arrives.
6. **Patterns compress delivery.** Most of the quarter a use case used to take
   was rebuilding decisions somebody had already made.
7. **Translate, do not insist.** Delivery time, operational risk, scalability,
   reliability, cost, business impact. That is the language an engineering
   investment has to be expressed in before anyone can fund it.
8. **Teach every week.** A show and tell costs nothing and is the only real
   insurance a small team has against key person dependency.
9. **Build yourself out of the critical path on purpose.** Solve, pattern,
   teach, federate, step back.

## The thing I did not expect to learn

The most valuable thing I took from this had very little to do with any tool.

I learned how to build a capability where the structure was not already there.
That meant working when roles were not neatly defined, resources were limited,
priorities competed, production was on fire, and most of the people I needed did
not report to me.

It forced skills I did not expect to need. Finding the right people and building
real relationships with them. Negotiating for resources. Managing expectations
honestly. Balancing this quarter against next year. Explaining one technical
problem three ways for three audiences. Knowing when to take ownership and when
to give it away.

Tools change. Databricks will change, the databases will change, the frameworks
certainly will. Walking into an unstructured environment, working out what is
missing and building something that eventually runs without you is the part that
keeps its value.

There is a moment in One Piece where Dr Hiluluk asks when a person truly dies,
and answers that it is not when they are shot, but when they are forgotten.
Systems are how a team remembers.

None of this required genius. It required diagnosing the system rather than the
people, doing the unglamorous work in an order that made sense, and accepting
that the most valuable things I built are the ones that no longer need me.

The goal of technical leadership is not to become indispensable to daily
operations. It is the opposite. Build the systems, the patterns and the
capability, then make sure they keep working when you are not in the room. Build
systems that outlive the people who built them, including you.
