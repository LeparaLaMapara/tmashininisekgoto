# Building a Data Science Capability in Under Two Years: A Real Life Roadmap

> I did not build a data science team from scratch. The team was already there,
> and the people in it were carrying real production work for real parts of the
> business. What was not there was the system around them. There was no handover.
> There was no useful documentation. Models were already running in production,
> and I had to work out what they did while helping keep them running. This is
> the story of building the capability around a team that already existed, in
> under two years, including the parts I got wrong.

## What I walked into

Let me be exact about the starting point, because everything else follows from
it.

There was no handover. There was no useful documentation for the systems and
models I inherited. Not thin documentation. Not out of date documentation. None
that helped.

Meanwhile the work was live. Models were running in production. The environment
was handling roughly five million records a day. Parts of the business were
making decisions using the output. Some problems were already happening. So I
had to learn what existed at the same time as helping keep it working.

The knowledge did exist. It just lived inside people's heads. If you wanted to
know how a model worked, or what fed it, or why someone had made a particular
choice two years ago, you had to find the right person and hope they remembered.
Standards were inconsistent, because everyone had solved the same problems in
their own way. Nobody could give you a straight answer to simple questions:
what do we have, who owns it, how does it work, and why was it built like that?

The team was not underperforming. The people were good. The system around them
was incomplete. That distinction matters, because if you read it backwards you
start replacing people, and you end up with a new team inside the same broken
system, with less memory of how anything works than before.

**Having a data science team is not the same thing as having a data science
capability.**

A team is people. A capability is those people plus the platform, the data
foundation, the practices and the standards that let them deliver again and
again without heroics. We had the first. We had to build the second.

| What I inherited | Where it is now |
| --- | --- |
| No handover, no useful documentation | Written down, and used by other teams |
| Requests arriving anywhere, worked on immediately | Requests enter one door and get prioritised |
| No dedicated data engineering | A data engineer in the team, specialists shared |
| Every model deployed its own way | Reusable patterns, five production models moved onto them |
| A stack built for running applications | Databricks for large scale data work |
| Roughly a quarter to get a use case live | Roughly two weeks, or a month with legacy systems |

None of that came from replacing people. It came from changing the system around
them, in an order that mattered.

## First, make the work visible

The first problem was not technical. Nobody, including me, could answer a simple
question: what is this team working on, and how did we decide that?

Work arrived directly. Someone who needed something asked the person they knew,
and it became work that afternoon, because saying yes is easier than having a
process that can say later. That produces a team which is permanently busy and
permanently behind, where the loudest request wins.

So we built a front door. Jira and two week sprints are the visible part, and
honestly the least interesting part. What mattered was the shape underneath. A
request now arrives in one place rather than in someone's inbox. It gets weighed
against everything the team has already promised. It goes into a list of agreed
work, and it gets picked up when there is room for it. Then everyone can see
where it is, including the person who asked.

![The demand flow: a request enters an intake, is prioritised against existing commitments, lands in the book of work, is pulled into a sprint and delivered, with visibility across every step](/posts/ds_demand_flow.png)

This is not process for its own sake. It changed what conversations were
possible. Before, the answer to "can you also do this" was yes, or an argument
with whoever pushed hardest. Now the answer is a comparison: here is what it
would push out, and here is what that costs. Choosing work stops being a
popularity contest and becomes a decision the business can actually make.

We kept the ceremony light. Standup every second day rather than daily, because
a small team fighting production issues does not need another meeting, it needs
a rhythm. At the end of each sprint, the honest questions: what got done, what
did not, why, and who is stuck.

The first thing visibility bought us was not speed. It was knowledge. Within a
few sprints I could see which skills we had, which we were missing, and exactly
where work went to die. You cannot decide what to build or who to hire until you
can see the work.

## Writing down what nobody had written down

Once the work was visible, the next problem showed itself. Almost everything
important was in somebody's head. In a small team that is not a quirk of
culture. It is a single point of failure with a resignation letter attached.

Confluence was the tool. Turning private knowledge into shared knowledge was the
job, and those are not the same project.

We started with the onboarding guide I wished someone had handed me: every tool
we use, where to find it, how to get access, what to request, and what training
a regulated environment requires before you touch anything. Then the harder
work, which was going back into our own production systems and working out what
they did. What each model does. How it works. What data feeds it. Who uses the
output. Then the deployment steps, the jobs that run every month, the patterns
we kept reusing, and a straight answer to the question that wastes more time
than any other: does this already exist somewhere?

Richard Feynman was once told that his notebooks were a record of his thinking.
He corrected the historian. The notes were not a record of the thinking, he
said. They were the thinking. That is what documentation is for a team. Not
admin done after the real work. The team thinking, in a form that survives the
team.

Then it paid off in a way I did not plan for.

Production models came under audit, compliance review and model governance.
Reviewers wanted to know what a model did, how it worked, what data it used, why
certain choices had been made, what controls existed and what evidence backed
any of it.

I want to be careful here. Writing something down does not make it compliant.
That is not the claim. The claim is smaller and more useful. Because we had
already done the work of understanding these systems and writing them down, we
were not starting from zero when somebody finally asked. We could hand over real
information and walk reviewers through the models instead of spending weeks
rediscovering our own production estate under pressure.

Months earlier, documentation had felt like extra work. During audit, it became
evidence.

## The infrastructure we actually had

It would be easy, and wrong, to say there was no infrastructure. There was
plenty.

We had Kubernetes. We had Jenkins and CI pipelines. We had Microsoft SQL Server
running on premises, PostgreSQL databases, Windows virtual machines, and the
usual application infrastructure around them. Some work also depended on
somebody's laptop, which is its own kind of risk. A few database environments
were not well looked after either, in ordinary ways: unclear permissions, unclear
access, unclear ownership, nobody obviously responsible for the housekeeping.

None of those technologies are bad. Kubernetes is not bad. Jenkins is not bad.
SQL Server and PostgreSQL are not bad. Virtual machines are not bad. I want to
be clear about that, because the lazy version of this story is a fight between
tools, and that is not what happened.

The problem was fit. Almost all of it had been designed around applications:
software that serves requests, keeps a service up, and touches a modest amount
of data at a time. Our work was going the other way. We needed to move large
amounts of data, process large datasets, rerun history when something changed,
train models, run experiments, and repeat all of that reliably.

Here is the simplest way I can say it:

**We had plenty of places to run software. What we did not have was a good place
to do data science at scale.**

Those are different problems, and they want different infrastructure.

## The month the records kept arriving

Then reality presented the bill for infrastructure decisions made long before I
arrived.

The business migrated to a new core policy administration environment. During
that migration things broke, and one of our production products started falling
behind. It could no longer process records at the rate they were arriving.

By the time it was contained, the backlog was roughly 250 million records,
against a normal daily volume of roughly five million. On the existing stack,
working through that took roughly three to four months.

The part people underestimate is that the data does not wait for you. It kept
arriving at the same rate the whole time. Every day we did not catch up, another
day of records joined the queue behind the ones already there.

```text
arriving faster than we process
   -> the backlog grows

same rate in, same rate out
   -> it stops growing
   -> but it never shrinks

processing faster than it arrives
   -> the backlog finally clears
```

You cannot fix that with discipline. It was not a tuning problem or a code
quality problem. The work was large scale data processing and the platform was
built for running applications. The two did not fit, and every hour we spent
being clever inside that limit was an hour spent proving the limit.

It was not just a technical inconvenience either. Clients complained. The
business pushed hard, and reasonably so. Cancellations were happening around the
wider issue. People on the team were under real stress for weeks.

I had seen this coming, and this is the most transferable thing I know:
**you do not get budget for infrastructure. You get budget for pain.**

When the backlog arrived I did not bring management a complaint. I brought a
costed proposal that was already written, and then I carried it through: the
commercial case, standing up the environment with the platform and networking
teams, and a route off the legacy stack that other workloads could follow later.

Once that workload moved into Databricks, a comparable volume could be processed
in under a day.

![The processing comparison: roughly 250 million records took three to four months on the old stack, and a comparable workload runs in under a day on Databricks](/posts/ds_backlog_processing.png)

I want to be careful with that comparison, because it is the strongest number
here and it would be easy to oversell. It is not a benchmark. It is the same
kind of work, running on infrastructure shaped for it instead of against it. The
argument about strategy ended there, which is the only way arguments like that
ever end.

## It was also a problem of trust

The migration did not only leave us behind on volume. It also introduced
inconsistencies in the data itself.

That matters more than it sounds. Some of those values feed calculations with
real financial consequences, including payouts. I am not claiming wrong payments
went out. I am saying it was a risk we had to get under control, and quickly.

So the problem was never simply "process the backlog". It was three things at
once. Clear the backlog. Keep up with everything still arriving each day. And
make sure the data can be trusted before anything downstream uses it.

That third one is what quietly set our engineering agenda. It is where the need
came from for proper ingestion and ETL, for checks that catch bad values before
they spread, for reconciliation so we could prove a number, for monitoring and
alerts so we heard about problems from our systems rather than from a client,
for logging and lineage so we could trace where a value came from, and for the
ability to reprocess history safely when we found something wrong.

None of that is a shopping list of platform features. Each one is an answer to a
question somebody was going to ask us.

**Scale is not only about processing more data. It is about processing more data
without losing control of it.**

## Why the fix had to start underneath

This is the central lesson of the whole story, and it took a crisis to make it
obvious to me.

Data science is not only analysis, notebooks, reports and models. Before anyone
can build a model worth trusting, somebody has to make sure the data can be
found, accessed, moved, understood, cleaned, transformed, checked, governed,
reproduced and monitored. If that layer is weak, everything above it is guessing
with extra steps.

I could have spent that period improving how we built models. It would not have
helped. The models were not the weak part.

**At scale, data science is only as reliable as the data foundation underneath
it.**

So we invested in data engineering, deliberately, as the thing that had to come
first.

## Where this thinking came from

It is fair to ask how I knew where to look.

Before this role I worked at IBM and Vodacom, in environments with much more
mature data science and data engineering practice than I found later. I saw
production data science that was genuinely engineered. I saw large scale data
processing treated as a discipline of its own. I saw deployment, automation and
reproducibility taken seriously, and technical roles working together rather
than throwing work over a wall.

I am not saying those places were perfect, and I am not saying I copied them.
The point is narrower than that. I had already seen what a more mature
capability looks like, so I had something to compare against.

That comparison is what let me read the situation correctly. Walking into no
handover, no documentation, infrastructure that did not fit, and a production
system falling behind, it would have been easy to treat each one as its own
emergency. Having seen the alternative, I could recognise them for what they
were. These were not model problems. They were capability problems.

## Databricks, and the first pattern we could reuse

I helped bring in Databricks as part of modernising the capability. Not because
it was fashionable, but because the work we were doing needed a platform built
for large scale data processing, and we had just been shown what happens when it
is not.

During the worst of the backlog pressure, I built the first version of something
we needed repeatedly: a reliable way to get data out of our on premises systems
and into the cloud data environment and catalog, using JDBC connections. Once
that worked, it was written down and taught, so the next person did not have to
solve it again from first principles.

That sequence is the actual pattern, and it matters more than the specific
technology:

```text
understand the problem
  -> design an approach
  -> build the first one
  -> prove it on real work
  -> write it down
  -> teach it
  -> the team adopts it
  -> repeat and improve it
  -> hand over ownership
```

The point was never for me to become the person who moves every dataset forever.
The point was to turn a problem we kept solving badly into something the team
could do repeatedly, and then get out of the way.

## Better practices above the platform

With a platform that fit the work and a data layer we could rely on, we could
finally improve how we did data science itself. We standardised how work was
structured and kept in source control, so a piece of analysis was something
another person could pick up and run. We improved reproducibility, and added
testing where it genuinely earned its place. We built repeatable ways to develop
models, deploy them, and move them from an experiment into production, with
CI/CD through GitHub workflows, and with governance, access control and
monitoring designed in rather than bolted on afterwards.

The order was not an accident:

```text
platform
   compute, storage, catalog
data engineering
   ingestion, ETL, quality
practices
   source control, testing, CI/CD
data science
   experiments, models, deployment
monitoring
   governance, alerts, lineage
```

Each layer bought room to improve the one above it. We could start making model
work repeatable instead of treating every project as a brand new engineering
problem.

## A capability is more than a platform

If you take one thing from this, take this. Buying Databricks does not give you
a data science capability, any more than buying a piano makes you a musician.

A capability is a stack, and most of it is not technology:

```text
           PEOPLE
   skills, trust, ownership
              |
          PRACTICES
   documentation, standards
      testing, CI/CD
              |
        DATA SCIENCE
   experiments, models
   deployment, monitoring
              |
      DATA ENGINEERING
   ingestion, ETL, quality
    pipelines, lineage
              |
          PLATFORM
   compute, storage, catalog
       access, cloud
```

Take any layer out and the ones above it wobble. That is why the tool decision
was the smallest part of this story, even though it is the part people ask about
most.

## Building people, not only technology

Budget was limited. We could not hire a finished specialist for every gap, and
even with money I am not sure we should have.

One of the most valuable people in this story already worked in the
organisation. They had strong data management knowledge and, more importantly,
they knew the place: where data lived, which databases mattered, how the
business actually used them. They then grew into the data engineering role. That
combination, real knowledge of the organisation plus a genuine appetite to
develop, was worth more to us than hiring a stronger generalist from outside,
because the scarce thing was context, not raw skill.

We also brought in a junior data scientist, and that was about capacity rather
than seniority. A large part of our demand was recurring, ad hoc analysis:
genuinely valuable, effectively endless, and quietly fatal to any sustained
production work. Giving that a proper home created room for everyone else.

The habit that holds all of this together is a show and tell every Friday, and
the rules are loose on purpose. Bring the model you are building. Bring
something you learned about the business. Bring a technology you are curious
about, a lesson from a course, a practice you want to argue for, an experiment
that worked, or one that did not. Failures count, and saying so out loud is most
of what makes it work. The purpose is to move knowledge sideways, so that
expertise stops collecting in one person, and it costs nothing.

## Deciding what to own and what to share

There is a difference between building capability and hoarding it.

We did not try to permanently own every skill we occasionally needed. That is
not only a budget argument. Keeping a specialist you need twice a year inside a
small team is poor design on its own terms. It creates expertise with nothing to
do and quietly duplicates a function the organisation already has.

So where we needed deep specialist help and a permanent hire was not justified,
we worked with the central technology teams and brought people in for the
specific problem: platform engineering, data engineering, AI engineering,
solution architecture and the central AI capability.

![The capability model: a core team of data science, data engineering and analysis, alongside federated central capability in platform engineering, AI engineering, solution architecture and central AI](/posts/ds_capability_mix.png)

Some things we built early only because nobody else was going to. That is fine
at the time and a bad permanent arrangement, because a small data science team
maintaining its own engineering platform is a small data science team that has
stopped doing data science. So we handed those things outward on purpose, to the
teams whose job they properly are.

Deciding that split is a real part of the job. Own everything and you build a
silo you cannot sustain. Share everything and you have no capability of your own.
It also happens to be how a pattern survives you, because anything that lives
inside one team dies when that team is reorganised, and teams are always
reorganised.

## Where Ubunye Engine fitted

[Ubunye Engine](https://ubunye-ai-ecosystems.github.io/ubunye_engine/) is an open
source engine I built during my PhD research on distributed systems. You describe
a data or machine learning pipeline once, as configuration and a small amount of
Python, and run that same description in different places.

I am aware of how that looks. A manager who builds something and then introduces
it to his own team is one bad decision away from vanity engineering. So the test
was simple. It had to solve a problem the team already had, and it had to survive
other engineers looking at it properly.

The problem it speaks to is the one in the section above. We needed one standard
way to build things instead of a new custom answer for every use case. The ideas
I had worked out while building Ubunye, about repeatability and running the same
work in different environments, shaped how I approached that, and helped shape
some of the patterns we adopted as a team.

There was a second reason that mattered as much. The team came from analytics and
data science backgrounds rather than platform engineering. Telling that team to
adopt strong engineering conventions in the abstract achieves very little. Giving
them a concrete pattern to work inside, while we build the skills alongside it,
achieves a great deal. Conventions teach.

Databricks and Ubunye are complementary, and the boundary is worth stating
plainly. Databricks is the platform: the compute, the storage, the catalog.
Ubunye is a way of writing pipelines that can run across environments. It does
not replace Databricks, platform engineering, Kubernetes or CI/CD, and any
explanation that suggests otherwise is selling you something.

Five production models have been modernised onto the newer patterns so far. The
number I care about is not how many models run in production. It is that existing
production work is actively moving onto something standard and maintainable,
instead of piling up.

## What changed in delivery

Numbers with their caveats attached, because these are observations, not
promises.

Getting a use case properly into production used to take roughly a quarter. Very
little of that quarter was the data science. It was engineering and integration
that somebody had already done before, being done again.

With the newer patterns, a reasonably straightforward use case without heavy
legacy integration typically moves from prototype toward production in roughly
two weeks. Where serious legacy integration is involved, closer to a month. The
variable is almost always the integration, not the modelling.

Recently we delivered at least three use cases inside a single month. Through the
old approach, a comparable set of work could have taken a significant part of a
year. I am not going to turn that into a saving figure, because the honest claim
is about order of magnitude, not accuracy to the rand.

The change I value most is not on that list. The team stopped being reactive. We
used to chase whatever was on fire that day. We now choose most of our work.

## Learning to influence instead of insisting I was right

The hardest part of these two years was not technical, and the way I used to tell
this story was wrong.

I used to frame it as holding a line against management. That version is
flattering and useless. What is actually happening is that two groups are working
to different clocks. Management needs delivery it can measure this quarter, which
is a fair requirement and not an obstacle. Engineering can see investments whose
returns arrive over years. Both views are legitimate. Neither is complete alone.

My job was not to win. It was to translate. An infrastructure investment does not
become fundable because it is architecturally correct. It becomes fundable when
it is described in terms the organisation can weigh: delivery time, operational
risk, reliability, cost and business impact.

Sometimes translating changed my own mind, and the answer that survived a cost
constraint was better than the one I walked in with. Other times it meant waiting
and gathering evidence until the investment was obvious to everyone rather than
only to me. Databricks is the clearest example. I did not win that argument by
being right about distributed processing. I won it when the pain had a number
attached, and I already had the proposal written.

## Building myself out of the critical path

Now the part I got wrong.

For a stretch, I was holding too much of it together personally: the process, the
technical direction, the engineering decisions, the delivery. It felt like
commitment. It was actually a bottleneck with good intentions, and it put a
ceiling on the team that had nothing to do with their ability.

The way out was to move deliberately through stages:

![The leadership progression: solve the problem, turn it into a pattern, teach the pattern, federate it into the wider organisation, then step out of the critical path](/posts/ds_leadership_stages.png)

Solve the problem. Turn the solution into a pattern. Teach the pattern. Connect
it into the wider organisation. Then step back, so the work no longer needs you.

We are not at the final stage everywhere, and I want to be honest about that.
Some things are close. Some are still at stage one. Knowing which is which is
most of the job. Bringing in a project manager and scrum masters to run the
process was part of this, and so was handing platform work to platform people.
The cost of this stage is ego, and it is the cheapest price on this page once you
understand what it buys.

I used to think the point of leading was being the person who could always fix
it. I now think it is the opposite. The measure of the job is whether the team
can say they did it themselves and be right about it.

## What I would carry into the next team

1. **Diagnose the system before you judge the people.** Good people inside an
   incomplete system look exactly like a weak team.
2. **Make the work visible first.** You cannot prioritise what you cannot see,
   and you cannot plan skills or hiring until the work is in the open.
3. **Write things down while you learn them.** You are not doing admin. You are
   building the only version of the knowledge that survives someone leaving.
4. **Check whether your infrastructure fits your problem.** Good tools aimed at
   the wrong shape of work will still fail you.
5. **Fix the foundation before the models.** At scale, data science is only as
   reliable as the data underneath it.
6. **Write the costed proposal before the crisis.** Funding follows visible
   pain, so have the answer ready for the day the pain arrives.
7. **Patterns are what compress delivery.** Most of that old quarter was
   rebuilding decisions somebody had already made.
8. **Decide what to own, develop and share.** All three, on purpose.
9. **Grow the people you have.** Knowledge of the organisation is harder to hire
   than technical skill.
10. **Build yourself out of the critical path deliberately.** Solve, pattern,
    teach, share, step back.

## The thing I did not expect to learn

The most valuable thing I took from this had very little to do with any tool.

I learned how to build a capability in an environment where the structure was
not already there. That meant working when roles were not neatly defined,
resources were limited, priorities competed with each other, production was on
fire, and most of the people I needed did not report to me.

It forced me to develop skills I did not expect to need. Working out what was
actually broken rather than what was loudest. Finding the right people and
building real relationships with them. Asking for resources and negotiating for
them. Managing expectations honestly. Balancing this quarter against next year.
Explaining the same technical problem in three different ways for three
different audiences. Knowing when to take ownership, and when to give it away.
Creating enough structure for people to work well, without creating bureaucracy
nobody wanted.

I did not expect that to become one of the most useful things I would take from
the experience. It did.

Tools change. Databricks will change, the databases will change, the frameworks
certainly will. Walking into an unstructured environment, working out what is
missing, bringing the right people together and building something that
eventually runs without you is the part that keeps its value.

There is a moment in One Piece where Dr Hiluluk asks when a person truly dies,
and answers that it is not when they are shot, but when they are forgotten.
Systems are how a team remembers.

None of this required genius. It required diagnosing the system rather than the
people, doing the unglamorous work in a sensible order, and accepting that the
most valuable things I built are the ones that no longer need me. Build systems
that outlive the people who built them, including you.
