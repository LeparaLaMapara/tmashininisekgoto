# Building a Data Science Capability in Under Two Years: A Real Life Roadmap

> I did not build a data science team from scratch. The team was already there,
> and the people in it were carrying real production work. What was not there was
> the system around them. There was no handover. There was no useful
> documentation. Models were already running in production, and I had to work out
> what they did while helping keep them running. This is the story of building
> the capability around a team that already existed, and what I would do again.

## What I walked into

There was no handover. There was no useful documentation for the systems and
models I inherited. Not thin documentation. Not out of date documentation. None.

The work was live. Models were running in production. The environment was
handling roughly five million records a day. Parts of the business were making
decisions with the output. Some problems were already happening. So I had to
learn what existed while helping keep it working.

The knowledge did exist. It just lived inside people's heads. If you wanted to
know how a model worked, or why someone chose something two years ago, you had
to find the right person and hope they remembered.

In that situation the temptation is to start fixing the loudest thing. Do not.
**The loudest thing is almost never the load bearing thing.**

The team was not underperforming. The people were good. The system around them
was incomplete. Read that backwards and you start replacing people, and end up
with a new team inside the same broken system.

**Having a data science team is not the same thing as having a data science
capability.** A team is people. A capability is those people plus the platform,
the data foundation, the practices and the standards that let them deliver again
and again without heroics.

Inheriting systems that only lived in someone's memory taught me the idea this
whole story runs on: build systems that outlive the people who built them.
Everything below is me trying to make sure the next person did not inherit the
same darkness I did.

| What I inherited | Where it is now |
| --- | --- |
| No handover, no useful documentation | Written down, and used by other teams |
| Requests arriving anywhere, worked on immediately | One front door, priorities agreed |
| No dedicated data engineering | A data engineer in the team, specialists shared |
| Every model deployed its own way | Reusable patterns, five production models moved onto them |
| A stack built for running applications | A platform built for large scale data work |
| Roughly a quarter to get a use case live | Roughly two weeks, or a month with legacy systems |

## Make the work visible

Nobody, including me, could answer a simple question: what is this team working
on, and how did we decide that?

Work arrived directly. Someone who needed something asked the person they knew,
and it became work that afternoon. That produces a team which is permanently
busy and permanently behind, where the loudest request wins.

So we built a front door. [Jira](https://www.atlassian.com/software/jira) and two
week sprints are the visible part and the least interesting part. What mattered
was the shape underneath. A request arrives in one place. It gets weighed against
what the team already promised. It goes into an agreed list, and it gets picked
up when there is room.

![The demand flow: a request enters an intake, is prioritised against existing commitments, lands in the book of work, is pulled into a sprint and delivered, with visibility across every step](/posts/ds_demand_flow.png)

This changed what conversations were possible. Before, the answer to "can you
also do this" was yes, or an argument with whoever pushed hardest. Now it is a
comparison: here is what it would push out, and here is what that costs.

What I did not expect was that visibility is what makes a team self managing. A
sprint is not a list of twenty tasks with hourly updates. It is a shared
direction, a time window and agreed priorities. Once people can see the work,
they know what they own, they pace themselves, they ask for help when stuck, and
they help each other without being told to. Nobody has to chase anybody.

That only worked because my manager gave us room. He trusted the team and did not
try to run the implementation from above. It is very hard to build an independent
team if the people above it will not let go, and I would not want this story read
as though I did it alone.

## Write down what nobody wrote down

Almost everything important was in somebody's head. In a small team that is not a
quirk of culture. It is a single point of failure with a resignation letter
attached.

[Confluence](https://www.atlassian.com/software/confluence) was the tool. Turning
private knowledge into shared knowledge was the job.

We started with the onboarding guide I wished someone had handed me: every tool,
where to find it, how to get access, what training a regulated environment
requires before you touch anything. Then the harder work of going back into our
own production systems and working out what they did. What each model does. What
data feeds it. Who uses the output. And a straight answer to the question that
wastes the most time: does this already exist somewhere?

Richard Feynman was once told his notebooks were a record of his thinking. He
corrected the historian. The notes were not a record of the thinking. They were
the thinking. That is what documentation is for a team.

Then it paid off in a way I did not plan for. Production models came under audit,
compliance review and model governance. Reviewers wanted to know what a model
did, what data it used, why choices were made, and what evidence backed any of
it. Writing something down does not make it compliant, and that is not the claim.
The claim is smaller. We were not starting from zero when somebody finally asked.

Months earlier, documentation had felt like extra work. During audit, it became
evidence.

## The infrastructure we actually had

It would be easy, and wrong, to say there was no infrastructure.

We had [Kubernetes](https://kubernetes.io/). We had [Jenkins](https://www.jenkins.io/)
and CI pipelines. We had [Microsoft SQL Server](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sql/sql-server/)
on premises, [PostgreSQL](https://www.postgresql.org/) databases, Windows virtual
machines. Some work depended on somebody's laptop, which is its own risk. A few
database environments were not well looked after in ordinary ways: unclear
permissions, unclear ownership, nobody obviously responsible for housekeeping.

None of those technologies are bad. The lazy version of this story is a fight
between tools, and that is not what happened.

The problem was fit. Almost all of it was designed around applications: software
that serves requests, stays up, and touches a modest amount of data at a time.
Our work went the other way. We needed to move and process large amounts of data,
rerun history when something changed, train models and repeat all of it reliably.

**We had plenty of places to run software. What we did not have was a good place
to do data science at scale.**

## The month the records kept arriving

Then reality presented the bill for infrastructure decisions made before I
arrived.

The business migrated to a new core policy administration environment. Things
broke, and one of our production products fell behind. It could not process
records at the rate they arrived.

By the time it was contained, the backlog was roughly 250 million records,
against a normal daily volume of roughly five million. On the existing stack,
working through that took roughly three to four months.

We first tried the obvious thing, which was to make the existing setup go faster.
It was not enough. That is the useful part of the story, because it proved the
problem was not effort or tuning. The work was large scale data processing and
the platform was built for running applications.

The part people underestimate is that the data does not wait for you.

```text
arriving faster than we process
   -> the backlog grows

same rate in, same rate out
   -> it stops growing
   -> but it never shrinks

processing faster than it arrives
   -> the backlog finally clears
```

It was not only a technical inconvenience. Clients complained. The business
pushed hard, and reasonably so. Cancellations were happening around the wider
issue. People were under real stress for weeks.

I had seen it coming, and this is the most transferable thing I know:
**you do not get budget for infrastructure. You get budget for pain.**

So when the backlog arrived I did not bring management a complaint. I brought a
costed proposal that was already written, then carried it through: the commercial
case, standing up the environment with the platform and networking teams, and a
route off the legacy stack that other workloads could follow.

Once that workload moved into [Databricks](https://www.databricks.com/), a
comparable volume could be processed in under a day.

![The processing comparison: roughly 250 million records took three to four months on the old stack, and a comparable workload runs in under a day on Databricks](/posts/ds_backlog_processing.png)

That is not a benchmark. It is the same kind of work, running on infrastructure
shaped for it instead of against it.

## Trust, quality and control

The migration did not only leave us behind on volume. It introduced
inconsistencies in the data itself.

That matters more than it sounds. Some of those values feed calculations with
real financial consequences, including payouts. I am not claiming wrong payments
went out. I am saying it was a risk we had to control quickly.

So the problem was never simply "process the backlog". It was three things at
once: clear the backlog, keep up with what still arrives daily, and make sure the
data can be trusted before anything downstream uses it.

That third one set our engineering agenda. It is where the need came from for
proper ingestion and ETL, checks that catch bad values before they spread,
reconciliation so we could prove a number, monitoring and alerts so we heard
about problems from our systems rather than from a client, and the ability to
safely reprocess history.

The same pressure applied to control. A regulated environment asks questions that
compute alone cannot answer. Who can access this data? Where did it come from?
What happened to it on the way? Did the quality checks pass? Which version
produced this result? Can we reproduce it, explain it, and safely run it again?

Governance is not paperwork you add at the end. It is a set of questions your
system should be able to answer on any given Tuesday.

**Scale is not only about processing more data. It is about processing more data
without losing control of it.**

## The foundation underneath

This is the central lesson, and it took a crisis to make it obvious to me.

Data science is not only analysis, notebooks and models. Before anyone can build
a model worth trusting, somebody has to make sure the data can be found,
accessed, moved, understood, cleaned, checked, governed, reproduced and
monitored. If that layer is weak, everything above it is guessing with extra
steps.

I could have spent that period improving how we built models. It would not have
helped. The models were not the weak part.

**At scale, data science is only as reliable as the data foundation underneath
it.**

It is fair to ask how I knew where to look. Before this role I worked at IBM and
Vodacom, in environments with much more mature data science and data engineering
practice. I saw production data science that was genuinely engineered, large
scale data processing treated as a discipline, and deployment and reproducibility
taken seriously. I am not saying those places were perfect, and I did not copy
them. The point is narrower. I had seen what a more mature capability looks like,
so I had something to compare against. That comparison is what let me read the
situation correctly. These were not model problems. They were capability
problems.

## The first pattern we could reuse

During the worst of the backlog pressure I built the first version of something
we needed repeatedly: a reliable way to get data out of our on premises systems
into the cloud data environment and catalog over
[JDBC](https://docs.oracle.com/javase/tutorial/jdbc/). Once it worked, it was
written down and taught, so the next person did not solve it again from scratch.

That sequence matters more than the technology:

```text
understand the problem
  -> design an approach
  -> build the first one
  -> prove it on real work
  -> write it down
  -> teach it
  -> the team adopts it
  -> repeat and improve it
  -> hand over ownership
```

The point was never for me to become the person who moves every dataset forever.
If I solve something once, the team should not need me to solve it again.

With a platform that fit and a data layer we could rely on, we could improve how
we did data science itself. Work went into source control so another person could
pick it up and run it. Reproducibility improved, with testing where it earned its
place. We built repeatable ways to develop models, deploy them and move them from
experiment into production, with CI/CD through
[GitHub Actions](https://docs.github.com/en/actions), and with access control and
monitoring designed in rather than bolted on.

That same foundation now carries a wide range of work: ingestion, ETL,
transformation, model workloads, deployment and orchestration, and increasingly
LLM and agent workloads too. The point is not the list. It is that one foundation
can carry many kinds of data and AI work, which is exactly what you lose when
every project invents its own.

## A capability is more than a platform

Buying Databricks does not give you a data science capability, any more than
buying a piano makes you a musician.

```text
           PEOPLE
   skills, trust, ownership
              |
          PRACTICES
   documentation, standards
      testing, CI/CD
              |
        DATA SCIENCE
   experiments, models
   deployment, monitoring
              |
      DATA ENGINEERING
   ingestion, ETL, quality
    pipelines, lineage
              |
          PLATFORM
   compute, storage, catalog
       access, cloud
```

Take any layer out and the ones above it wobble. That is why the tool decision
was the smallest part of this story, even though it is the part people ask about
most.

## Where Ubunye Engine fitted

[Ubunye Engine](https://ubunye-ai-ecosystems.github.io/ubunye_engine/) did not
start at work. It began in my own research, where I kept rebuilding the same
scaffolding for every experiment and got tired of it, and I carried on developing
it independently afterwards. It is open source and available on
[PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/ubunye-engine/). I mention the history only
because provenance is worth being accurate about.

Later, solving professional data engineering problems, I recognised that some of
the same ideas helped: reproducibility, portability, configuration, conventions,
and not doing one off work twice. The ideas I had worked out building it shaped
how I approached our problem, and helped shape some of the patterns we adopted as
a team.

A manager who builds something and introduces it to his own team is one bad
decision away from vanity engineering, so the test was simple. It had to solve a
problem the team already had, and survive other engineers looking at it properly.

It is worth keeping two things apart. A **platform** is compute, storage, catalog
and execution. A **framework** is the conventions and reusable ways you structure
and run work on top of it. Databricks is the first. Ubunye is the second. It does
not replace Databricks, platform engineering or CI/CD, and any explanation that
suggests otherwise is selling you something.

Five production models have been modernised onto the newer patterns so far. The
number I care about is not how many models run in production, but that existing
work is moving onto something standard instead of piling up.

## People, relationships and teaching

Budget was limited. We could not hire a finished specialist for every gap.

One of the most valuable people in this story already worked in the organisation.
They had strong data management knowledge and, more importantly, they knew the
place: where data lived, which databases mattered, how the business used them.
Given the opportunity, engineering direction, support and real responsibility,
they grew into the data engineering role. Building capability means developing
people, not only installing technology.

We also brought in a junior data scientist, for capacity rather than seniority. A
large part of our demand was recurring ad hoc analysis: valuable, endless, and
quietly fatal to sustained production work. Giving it a home created room for
everyone else.

For everything beyond that, I built relationships instead of headcount. Platform
engineers, data engineers, engineering specialists and technical leaders
elsewhere who had solved similar problems. We lined up on shared technical and
business goals, and that made it possible to pull in real expertise when we
needed it. **You do not need everyone to report to you to build something
together.** What makes that work is not process. It is trust, honest
communication and being technically credible enough that people want to help.

Then we taught. Databricks, [PySpark](https://spark.apache.org/docs/latest/api/python/),
the engineering patterns, the documentation habit, deployment. The Friday show
and tell is where a lot of it landed, and the rules are loose on purpose. Bring
the model you are building, something you learned about the business, a practice
you want to argue for, an experiment that worked, or one that did not. Failures
count. The purpose is to move knowledge sideways so that expertise stops
collecting in one person, and it costs nothing.

## Giving it away

There is a difference between building capability and hoarding it.

We built some of these things only because there was a gap and the business
needed them filled. That is fine at the time and a bad permanent arrangement,
because a small data science team maintaining its own engineering platform is a
small data science team that has stopped doing data science.

So as the patterns matured we moved them outward, to the technology, data
engineering and platform teams who should own them long term.
[Team Topologies](https://teamtopologies.com/) has the cleanest language for
this: platform teams exist so that other teams can flow. It also happens to be
how a pattern survives you, because anything living inside one team dies when
that team is reorganised, and teams are always reorganised.

![The capability model: a core team of data science, data engineering and analysis, alongside federated central capability in platform engineering, AI engineering, solution architecture and central AI](/posts/ds_capability_mix.png)

**Good technical leadership is not owning everything forever. Sometimes you build
what is missing, prove it, teach it, and then give it to the team that should own
it.**

I had to learn that on myself first. For a stretch I was holding too much
together personally: the process, the technical direction, the delivery. It felt
like commitment. It was a bottleneck with good intentions, and it put a ceiling
on the team that had nothing to do with their ability.

![The leadership progression: solve the problem, turn it into a pattern, teach the pattern, federate it into the wider organisation, then step out of the critical path](/posts/ds_leadership_stages.png)

Bringing in a project manager and scrum masters to run the process was part of
this, and so was handing platform work to platform people. The cost of this stage
is ego, and it is the cheapest price on this page.

## Where we are now

We are not finished, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Some foundational
ownership is still moving toward the teams that should hold it. Some things are
close. Some are still at stage one.

What has changed is real, though. Getting a use case properly into production
used to take roughly a quarter, and very little of that was the data science. It
was engineering somebody had already done before, being done again. Now a
reasonably straightforward use case moves from prototype toward production in
roughly two weeks, or closer to a month where serious legacy integration is
involved. Recently we delivered at least three use cases inside a single month.
Through the old approach that could have taken a significant part of a year. I am
not turning that into a savings figure, because the honest claim is about order
of magnitude.

The hardest part was never technical. I used to frame it as holding a line
against management, which is flattering and useless. Two groups were working to
different clocks. Management needed delivery it could measure this quarter, which
is fair. Engineering could see investments that pay back over years. My job was
not to win but to translate, because an infrastructure investment does not become
fundable by being architecturally correct. It becomes fundable when it is
described in delivery time, risk, reliability and cost. Sometimes that changed my
own mind, and the answer that survived a cost constraint was better than the one I
walked in with.

The change I value most is not on any of those lists. Our data scientists now
work on this platform and have adopted the patterns and the thinking. It is
increasingly how the team works rather than how I think we should work.

## The roadmap, in order

1. Understand what you inherited before you change it.
2. Make the work visible. You cannot prioritise what you cannot see.
3. Document what matters while you are learning it.
4. Find the real bottleneck, not the loudest one.
5. Fix the data foundation before the models.
6. Choose infrastructure that fits the workload you actually have.
7. Turn one off solutions into repeatable patterns.
8. Improve how data science itself is engineered, once the base is stable.
9. Build quality, monitoring and governance in rather than on.
10. Teach continuously, so knowledge stops living in one head.
11. Build relationships outside your reporting line.
12. Give people ownership instead of managing their hours.
13. Move mature responsibilities to the teams that should own them.
14. Keep going. It is not finished.

## The thing I did not expect to learn

The most valuable thing I took from this had very little to do with any tool.

I learned how to build a capability where the structure was not already there.
That meant working when roles were not neatly defined, resources were limited,
priorities competed, production was on fire, and most of the people I needed did
not report to me.

It forced skills I did not expect to need. Working out what was actually broken
rather than what was loudest. Finding the right people and building real
relationships. Negotiating for resources. Managing expectations honestly.
Balancing this quarter against next year. Explaining one technical problem three
different ways for three different audiences. Knowing when to take ownership, and
when to give it away.

Tools change. Databricks will change, the databases will change, the frameworks
certainly will. Walking into an unstructured environment, working out what is
missing, bringing the right people together and building something that
eventually runs without you is the part that keeps its value.

There is a moment in One Piece where Dr Hiluluk asks when a person truly dies,
and answers that it is not when they are shot, but when they are forgotten.
Systems are how a team remembers.

None of this required genius. It required diagnosing the system rather than the
people, doing the unglamorous work in a sensible order, and accepting that the
most valuable things I built are the ones that no longer need me. Build systems
that outlive the people who built them, including you.
